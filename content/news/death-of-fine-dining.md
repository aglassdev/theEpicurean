Last week, chef Bruno Verjus announced that this December, La Table in Paris will be closing, which he claims is by his personal choice only. He took this announcement as a platform to declare to the world that "fine dining is dead." A chef as awarded as him, with two Michelin Stars and two consecutive Top 10 finishes in The50Best surely knows what he is talking about...right? If you take about two seconds to look behind the thin layer of bullshit in his words, you can see the much bleaker truth about his current standing, and more importantly, about his blatant hypocrisy.

First, let’s start with his restaurant's closure. It's really not a huge surprise. Restaurant turnover has been extreme for years, they are notoriously fine-margin establishments operating in a cut-throat industry. Even in a holy site such as Paris, we have seen many fine dining restaurants shut their doors due to unprofitability. But Verjus's lack of revenue didn't come from higher food prices, or rent, or wages. No, it actually came from his own ego. His restaurant offered one tasting menu, priced at €480 per person, which is at the high end, even in fine dining, especially considering all nine three-star establishments in the city offer cheaper menus. As people realized that there are better value-for-money restaurants in the city, fewer and fewer seats got filled at La Table, and Bruno couldn't afford to set it anymore.

To cover for his complete failure of management and to pad his ego on his fall, he blamed the closure on a general death of haute cuisine, claiming it is unsustainable and losing its soul. Everyone with a brain came back at him with a storm of criticism, forcing him to scurry back into his Parisian hole. He reemerged within the day, revising his claim to “fine dining at La Table is dead.” All of a sudden, Verjus wants to feed the world, not the 1% that he has consciously chosen to accept by pricing his restaurant the way he has.

Moving past the chef, it has opened up a broader discussion into the question he proposed: Is fine dining dead? No, it is not. Food has never and will never truly die, because humans will always have an appreciation for art. And just like traditional art, food is subjective. Nine courses at Le Bernardin may feel like the usual Thursday dinner for one, but Dante's Inferno to another. Until the end of humanity, there will always be those who desire excellence in the culinary art, and there will always be those to push it further.

My only concession is that fine dining has lost its meaning a bit. The term has skewed off from what it’s supposed to mean. This can be exemplified by the "Summer" menu at Singapore's one-star Cherry Garden by Chef Fei: caviar, lobster, wagyu, and black truffle for $288. This isn't a menu with any narrative, it’s a list of buzz ingredients to entice idiots and influencers (not that there is much of a difference between those two). Fine dining is supposed to mean food of the highest quality and preparation, not the most expensive and extravagant. This is why I prefer the term gastronomy when discussing dining, as it provides a better meaning of what food can be, good regardless of price.
